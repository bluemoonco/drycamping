How to find free camping for your RV

Use official maps to shortlist a place, then check the details that decide whether your rig—and your trip—will fit.

Updated September 24, 2026 · DryCamping.com

A campsite pin is a lead, not permission

Before driving to any suggested location, confirm ownership, camping permission, current access, and restrictions with the responsible land manager. This guide points you to official resources; it does not verify individual campsites.

Start with the people who manage the land

Bureau of Land Management

Look for recreation areas and dispersed-camping guidance, then check the relevant field office’s current information. BLM also operates developed campgrounds, some with fees.

Explore BLM recreation areas ↗

U.S. Forest Service

Find the forest you want to visit and look for dispersed-camping information, alerts, closures, and motor vehicle use maps. A forest-wide map is the starting point for checking a specific route.

Find a national forest ↗

National Park Service

Read the individual park’s camping information. Park campgrounds may charge fees and require reservations; do not assume public land means free dispersed camping.

Find an NPS campground ↗

Recreation.gov

Use the federal reservation platform to check listed campgrounds and facilities. It is useful for backup options as well as planned stays, but it is not a complete directory of dispersed sites.

Build a shortlist, not a single destination

Pick a general area within your driving range, then identify two or three potential stops. Separate must-haves from preferences. Your must-haves might be an accessible road, enough space for a trailer, and an overnight temperature your equipment can handle. A lake view belongs on the preference list.

Keep an established campground as a fallback when possible. A full area, temporary closure, soft road, or tight turnaround is easier to deal with when you have already picked somewhere else to go.

Check six things before you drive

  1. The exact boundary. Nearby private or restricted parcels can sit beside public land. Confirm the intended campsite itself.
  2. Camping permission and time limits. Rules vary by location. Ask about the stay limit, permits, fees, and where camping is restricted.
  3. Current route conditions. Look for seasonal gates, washouts, surface restrictions, vehicle length limits, grades, and places to turn around.
  4. Fire and generator restrictions. Check current local notices shortly before arrival, rather than relying on an old review.
  5. Supply stops. Identify potable water, fuel, and an approved wastewater disposal point along your route.
  6. Your exit plan. Know where to go if weather changes, supplies run low, or the campsite is unsuitable.

Use reviews for context

A recent camper report can help you spot road problems or a noisy setting. It cannot establish that a stay is authorized. Treat photos and reviews as additional context, especially when the reviewer’s vehicle is much smaller than yours.

Save the official page, directions, and relevant map before you lose reception. If a detail matters to the trip and the page does not answer it, contact the local office.

Keep the place worth visiting

Choose an appropriate existing campsite, stay on authorized routes, and take your trash away. Keep RV wastewater in your tanks for approved disposal. Leave room for others and follow posted restrictions. A remote setting still deserves the same care as a maintained campground.

Before leaving, use the trip checklist and compare your trip length with your water and tank estimates.

Common questions

Can I camp anywhere on BLM land?

No. Check the exact location for camping restrictions, closures, access rules, and any local requirements.

Is dispersed camping always free?

Do not assume so. Check whether a permit, access pass, or fee applies to your chosen location and activity.

Can I stay for 14 days everywhere?

No. Stay limits and relocation requirements vary. Use the rule published by the responsible office for that specific area.
