Dry camping vs. boondocking

The terms overlap, but they describe different parts of a camping experience. Understanding the difference helps you know what to expect.

Updated September 24, 2026 · DryCamping.com

The quick answer

Dry camping describes the absence of RV hookups. Boondocking commonly describes camping without hookups away from developed campgrounds. People also use “boondocking” more broadly, so ask about the actual facilities and rules.

Three terms you will see

TermWhat it describesWhat it does not guarantee
Dry campingNo connection to electricity, water, or sewerFree camping, solitude, or permission to stay
BoondockingCommonly, self-contained camping in an undeveloped settingA universal legal definition or consistent facilities
Dispersed campingCamping outside developed recreation facilities, subject to the land manager’s rulesPermission to drive or camp anywhere

A few practical examples

A numbered campground site without hookups: this is dry camping even if there are toilets, a water spigot, and a nightly fee. It can be a useful first step because you can practice using onboard systems with some facilities nearby.

An authorized site on an undeveloped public-land route: this may be described as boondocking or dispersed camping. Expect to verify the boundaries, access, and restrictions yourself. Being on public land does not remove the need to check current local rules.

An overnight parking stop with permission: you may sleep without hookups, but parking permission is not always permission to set up chairs, open awnings, or run a generator. Ask the property owner and check applicable local requirements before treating it as a campsite.

Ask better questions than “Is it boondocking?”

  • Are electrical, water, or sewer connections provided at the site?
  • Is potable water available elsewhere, and is it operating?
  • Are there toilets, trash collection, and a dump station?
  • Are reservations, fees, or permits required?
  • What vehicles fit the access road and campsite?
  • What restrictions apply to fires, generators, and length of stay?

Your planning stays the same

Regardless of the label, make an inventory of your usable power, fresh water, and waste capacity. A forest view does not change your refrigerator’s demand, and a paid campsite does not guarantee a place to refill. Check facilities individually.

Use the battery calculator and water calculator before booking. Then work through the trip checklist. If you are just starting, a short stay without hookups is a practical way to learn before choosing a more remote location.

Common questions

Does “primitive campsite” mean no hookups?

Often, but site descriptions vary. Read the specific facilities list instead of relying on the label.

Can a dry campsite have drinking water nearby?

Yes. There can be a shared water source even when individual sites do not have water hookups. Confirm availability and whether it is potable.
